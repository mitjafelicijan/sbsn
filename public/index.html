AI-Generated Books Confront Publishing Industry With Big Questions

Nov 16, 2026 03:45 | Source: slashdot.org

Romance novelist Coral Hart publishes her own romance novels for the Kindle, and found a kind of superpowere when she realized "the algorithm favours those who can produce a new book at least once a month," reports The Guardian: Hart says she knows of authors who hire more than a dozen ghostwriters to keep pace. By herself, Hart can write 10,000 words a day, but AI could help her produce many more. Since 2025 she has written 370 novels under 21 pen names using AI, earning herself "high six figures". Like any Kindle Unlimited author, she is only paid per pages read: it would make no business sense for her to churn out books that readers quickly abandon... "So, it's not taking the human out of the equation. It's taking the friction and the time and the business blocks out of the way." One study (awaiting peer review), led by Tuhin Chakrabarty of Stony Brook University, estimates that around one fifth of recently self-published books contain at least 25% AI-generated text. Hart now teaches courses on how to use AI to produce books. Unless disclosing the use of AI becomes a legal requirement, she advises other authors not to do so because of the public response. "The minute someone discloses [AI use] they get death threats, they get called names, they're ousted," she says.... In the literary world, many people fear the tech will undercut and replace them. "I think people are very afraid of AI, and people in publishing are delusional in thinking that it's not going to overtake our industry, that their screaming on social media is going to stop it." But the anger comes from another place, too. If large language models can spit out books that people want to read, or produce a convincing pastiche of your favourite writer, that's almost certainly because they were trained on established writers' stolen work.... Many writers oppose AI for both moral and aesthetic reasons. One group of 70 prominent authors wrote an open letter calling on the big five publishers — Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster and Macmillan — to promise that they will "never release books that were created by a machine". The publishers did not promise. [Tess Callero, a literary agent with Europa Content, says] "...you have to wonder the reason they aren't. Is it because they see a future in which potentially these books could be monetised?"] So where do we go from here? Will big name writers win the argument for keeping AI out of literature, or will our bookshelves and e-readers soon be crammed full of AI-generated stories? And if a reader can't distinguish human from machine writing, does it still matter? The Guardian's author notes this new technology brings new challenges to the book publishing world: Publishers have concerns about US copyright law, "which specifies that a book must be 'human authored' and considers AI-generated text public domain. " The article notes high-profile books being canceled after accusations their writers used AI. But even in those cases, "Another unresolved question is if and when publishers should withdraw books," since if publishers liked it enough to make the deal, shouldn't they stand behind that judgment? And should there be a way for authors who want to experiment with AI to work with top book publishers? (One theory is publishing houses are afraid of alienating a handful of top writers who almost universally oppose AI.) "Right now, to write without the aid of a machine is to swim against a strong cultural tide," concludes the article's author, noting a recurring reaction from friends who "have looked at me with something close to pity when I tell them I would never use AI to write."
