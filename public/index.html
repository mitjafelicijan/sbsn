Just 400 US Data Centers Use More Electricity Than Michigan

The study, published Monday in Nature Sustainability, also finds that the majority of that electricity was generated using fossil fuels, and data center emissions are three times higher than they were before the AI boom. Federal and state governments do not track data center use separate from the rest of the power sector, despite mounting projections that data centers are putting serious strain on the American power grid. Individual data centers' power use is often considered proprietary business information, and data center operators and utilities do not have to disclose specific numbers to the public. The Harvard study specifically focuses on hyperscale data centers -- the largest types of facilities, which have become flashpoints in community opposition across the country. While often used for AI inference or training, not all of the hyperscalers surveyed in this study are being used for AI. [...] To assemble their dataset, the Harvard researchers put together a list of 403 hyperscale data centers around the country that were operational between May 2024 and April 2025 using a mix of industry research and satellite data. The team then modeled these facilities' energy use in a variety of scenarios and calculated the energy mix of the regional grids to estimate individual facilities' carbon emissions. Not all regions were created equal: Data centers in Virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa -- the first of which is home to Data Center Alley -- were responsible for more than 50 percent of the energy use in the study. The report notes that these findings may already be outdated. "Some of the massive campuses being proposed today are substantially larger than the typical facility in our dataset," says Gianluca Guidi, a postdoctoral research fellow at Harvard's T. H. Chan School of Public Health and the lead author of the study. The study also excludes onsite power generation, a rapidly growing trend that BloombergNEF estimates could increase overall U.S. emissions by 10% if all proposed projects are built. AT&T is suing (PDF) Comcast over its "fiber-powered" advertising, arguing that the term misleads consumers into believing Xfinity offers fiber-to-the-home service when most customers actually receive their Internet through coaxial cable. "Comcast should be required to restore to AT&T any and all profits earned as a result of its false and misleading advertising or provide AT&T with any other restitutionary relief the Court deems appropriate," the lawsuit said. The lawsuit asked for a permanent injunction "requiring Comcast to cease describing its hybrid fiber-coaxial service as 'Fiber-Powered.'" Ars Technica reports: While Comcast has deployed plenty of fiber throughout the US, its cable Internet service makes the final connection to homes with coaxial cable. This results in upload speeds being far slower than download speeds. In contrast, fiber-to-the-home service can provide symmetrical upload and download speeds. Comcast has deployed fiber-to-the-home in only small portions of its cable territory while advertising its service as fiber-powered in the majority of areas where cable makes the final connection. AT&T's lawsuit said that in describing its cable broadband as fiber-powered, Comcast "conveys to reasonable consumers the misleading impression that Xfinity Internet is a fiber-to-the-home service comparable to AT&T Fiber. In fact, the vast majority of Xfinity Internet runs over a hybrid fiber-coaxial ('HFC') network, where the critical 'last mile' connection to the customer's home uses copper coaxial cable, not fiber." [...] AT&T previously challenged Comcast's fiber-powered claims through the advertising industry's self-regulatory system operated by BBB National Programs' National Advertising Division (NAD). The NAD determined in June that Comcast accurately described its mix of cable and fiber technology in a disclosure, but that Comcast didn't make the disclosure prominently enough. [...] The NAD recommended that Comcast make the disclosure "more prominent and more proximate to the 'fiber-powered' Wi-Fi claim as necessary to ensure that the disclosure is clear and conspicuous." Comcast said it would comply with the decision, but AT&T's lawsuit makes it clear that it isn't satisfied with the current state of Comcast's advertising. TechCrunch reports: Artex is one of several open-source AI-powered penetration testing platforms, a category of software built to probe a network's defenses. Hackers can connect Artex to hosted frontier large language models (LLMs) like those from Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as to open-source models and non-U.S. models. The FBI says Artex intrusions can be "noisy," meaning they generate reams of traffic and data that may trigger a targeted company's defensive alerts. But the tools allow hackers to move at speeds that makes it far more harder for human defenders to keep up. In some cases, Artex-linked attacks can move from targeting a system to active compromise "in under three hours." Oftentimes, Artex will rely on rapidly scanning a victim's network to look for signs of weakness, and then exploit simple security bugs, such as weak protections and exposed or stolen passwords to gain a foothold. From there, Artex allows the hacker to spread across the network for databases, mailboxes, and other sensitive information. [...] After Artex was used in cyberattacks, the tool's developer made the software closed-source to prevent further attacks. But the FBI says the previous open-source version continues to circulate and remains "widely accessible" for use in cyberattacks. It's not only companies and targeted victims that have been compromised as a result of weakened security exploited by AI tools; the tools themselves have been exposed as well, potentially allowing outsiders to access powerful hacking capabilities. Last week, researchers found hundreds of publicly exposed Artex systems online, which had been placed on the internet without a password, likely by mistake or misconfiguration -- including the Artex system that was used to target South Korea. More than 80% of the affected gateway servers became unresponsive at the outage's worst point, and because the months-old change could not simply be rolled back, engineers spent roughly 15 hours developing and deploying a patch that restored the previous code path. The Register reports: According to a preliminary post incident report published by Microsoft on November 15, the defect was introduced in July 2026. The update looked fine at the time, Microsoft says, having passed load testing and been deployed without any impact. However, the testing wasn't good enough. Microsoft's report states: "Our load testing tended to focus on weaker hardware with fewer cores." The defect caused "lock contention inside an internal Windows process which caused the machine's resources (CPU & memory) to be exhausted." "The contention issue is worsened on machines with more cores and newer hardware." The fix containing the defect was prepared specifically for Microsoft 365 network gateway servers. Then two things happened at once. Teams failed over to the "primary" gateway that supports Exchange Online, creating a surge in load that was not, in itself, particularly unusual. At the same time, almost 14 percent of server capacity for the networking gateway was out of service, which wasn't a problem by itself either: it was on its way to being restored and, according to Microsoft, "the service had sufficient capacity headroom." Together, they pushed the gateway into the defect and chaos ensued. It was a classic cascading failure scenario. When a server loses connectivity, traffic gets routed to the next healthy endpoint. Bringing servers back to service didn't help -- they were immediately overwhelmed and fell over again. "Our normally automated recovery processes were not designed to restore service from this broad unresponsive state." A straightforward rollback was also not possible because the defect was months old. Instead, engineers developed patch for the patch or, in Microsoft's words, "an update that returned the code path to the previously known good state in Windows." AI-Generated Books Confront Publishing Industry With Big Questions

Romance novelist Coral Hart publishes her own romance novels for the Kindle, and found a kind of superpowere when she realized "the algorithm favours those who can produce a new book at least once a month," reports The Guardian: Hart says she knows of authors who hire more than a dozen ghostwriters to keep pace. By herself, Hart can write 10,000 words a day, but AI could help her produce many more. Since 2025 she has written 370 novels under 21 pen names using AI, earning herself "high six figures". Like any Kindle Unlimited author, she is only paid per pages read: it would make no business sense for her to churn out books that readers quickly abandon... "So, it's not taking the human out of the equation. It's taking the friction and the time and the business blocks out of the way." One study (awaiting peer review), led by Tuhin Chakrabarty of Stony Brook University, estimates that around one fifth of recently self-published books contain at least 25% AI-generated text. Hart now teaches courses on how to use AI to produce books. Unless disclosing the use of AI becomes a legal requirement, she advises other authors not to do so because of the public response. "The minute someone discloses [AI use] they get death threats, they get called names, they're ousted," she says.... In the literary world, many people fear the tech will undercut and replace them. "I think people are very afraid of AI, and people in publishing are delusional in thinking that it's not going to overtake our industry, that their screaming on social media is going to stop it." But the anger comes from another place, too. If large language models can spit out books that people want to read, or produce a convincing pastiche of your favourite writer, that's almost certainly because they were trained on established writers' stolen work.... Many writers oppose AI for both moral and aesthetic reasons. One group of 70 prominent authors wrote an open letter calling on the big five publishers — Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Simon & Schuster and Macmillan — to promise that they will "never release books that were created by a machine". The publishers did not promise. [Tess Callero, a literary agent with Europa Content, says] "...you have to wonder the reason they aren't. Is it because they see a future in which potentially these books could be monetised?"] So where do we go from here? Will big name writers win the argument for keeping AI out of literature, or will our bookshelves and e-readers soon be crammed full of AI-generated stories? And if a reader can't distinguish human from machine writing, does it still matter? The Guardian's author notes this new technology brings new challenges to the book publishing world: Publishers have concerns about US copyright law, "which specifies that a book must be 'human authored' and considers AI-generated text public domain. " The article notes high-profile books being canceled after accusations their writers used AI. But even in those cases, "Another unresolved question is if and when publishers should withdraw books," since if publishers liked it enough to make the deal, shouldn't they stand behind that judgment? And should there be a way for authors who want to experiment with AI to work with top book publishers? (One theory is publishing houses are afraid of alienating a handful of top writers who almost universally oppose AI.) "Right now, to write without the aid of a machine is to swim against a strong cultural tide," concludes the article's author, noting a recurring reaction from friends who "have looked at me with something close to pity when I tell them I would never use AI to write."
