School Phone Bans Are Gradually Improving Test Scores

Nov 17, 2026 19:00 | Source: slashdot.org

An anonymous reader quotes a report from The New York Times: School cellphone bans are beginning to modestly improve students' academic performance, according to the first large national analysis that compared students' test scores before and after the bans. Schools with strict bans -- requiring students to lock their phones in pouches all school day -- saw math and reading scores improve as if students had an additional week or two of school a year, the study found. It was published Tuesday by the Center for Education Policy Research at Harvard. U.S. students' test scores began falling over a decade ago. The drop coincided with the rise of smartphones, raising questions about their role in the decline. The evidence so far on the academic effects of phone bans has been mixed. But the new study indicates they are starting to have an influence. The study looked at 3.3 million students between 2021 and 2026. They were in third through 10th grade, attending 4,600 U.S. schools. Using scores on MAP tests, which track achievement from fall to spring, it compared the performance of students at schools that put in strict phone bans with those at similar schools that did not have such bans. The annual gains reversed about half the annual loss that an average student experienced in the years leading up to the pandemic. (Students lost even more ground during pandemic closures.) "The earlier studies on cellphone bans have been dismissed as implying small impacts, and in-school cellphone use has been ruled out as playing a big role in the decline," said Thomas Kane, a professor at the Harvard Graduate School of Education and an author of the study. BugMeNot Calls It Quits

Nov 17, 2026 17:30 | Source: slashdot.org

BugMeNot, the password-sharing service launched in 2003 to help users bypass website registration requirements, has shut down without explanation after more than two decades. All that's left on the website is a farewell message that reads: "Thanks to everyone who envisioned a better internet." heise online reports: Founded by Guy King, the service had been online since 2003: BugMeNot was a collection of login credentials for various online services. Volunteers submitted credentials they had created for different websites. The idea was simple: Nobody wants to create a full account and provide all their sensitive information just to use a service occasionally. Two decades ago, this privacy concern was more firmly embedded in the public consciousness, but it has since faded considerably. Back then, usernames and passwords were usually the only access controls, so people could easily submit those credentials. Multifactor authentication was still exotic, and passkeys were a distant prospect. The once-popular service lost its shine after just a few years. Service providers quickly discovered and blocked most of the credentials. They could also prevent users from saving logins for their services directly through BugMeNot, for example when those services were paid. More modern security mechanisms also emerged in recent years. As a result, credentials found on BugMeNot rarely worked. Most online providers also blocked the service's disposable email addresses under @bugmenot.com, making them rarely usable. Many people forgot about the site. Now its operators have shut it down without giving a reason. Additional discussions about BugMeNot's closure can be found on Reddit and Hacker News. Redict deprecated in favor of Valkey

Nov 17, 2026 16:20 | Source: lwn.net

Drew DeVault has announced that Redict, a fork of Redis that was created in response to Redis's 2024 license change, has been deprecated. He recommends switching to Valkey, the permissively licensed Redis fork supported by the Linux Foundation. The two projects are mutually compatible, so switching should be as simple as changing which package is installed and renaming the configuration file. I had hoped to rally the Redis diaspora around a copyleft initiative, to protect ourselves from future rug-pulls, and made Redict to serve this purpose. However, with the valkey project's success, and Redis's return to a copyleft license, Redict was not able to build a sustainable community. We recommend that Redict users switch to valkey, which uses a permissive license but has better long-term prospects. We advise against switching back to Redis; though it is once again open source, and copyleft, it uses a contributor license agreement which will facilitate another rug-pull when Redis has a bad financial quarter.

Federal and state governments do not track data center use separate from the rest of the power sector, despite mounting projections that data centers are putting serious strain on the American power grid. Individual data centers' power use is often considered proprietary business information, and data center operators and utilities do not have to disclose specific numbers to the public. The Harvard study specifically focuses on hyperscale data centers -- the largest types of facilities, which have become flashpoints in community opposition across the country. While often used for AI inference or training, not all of the hyperscalers surveyed in this study are being used for AI. [...] To assemble their dataset, the Harvard researchers put together a list of 403 hyperscale data centers around the country that were operational between May 2024 and April 2025 using a mix of industry research and satellite data. The team then modeled these facilities' energy use in a variety of scenarios and calculated the energy mix of the regional grids to estimate individual facilities' carbon emissions. Not all regions were created equal: Data centers in Virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa -- the first of which is home to Data Center Alley -- were responsible for more than 50 percent of the energy use in the study. The report notes that these findings may already be outdated. "Some of the massive campuses being proposed today are substantially larger than the typical facility in our dataset," says Gianluca Guidi, a postdoctoral research fellow at Harvard's T. H. Chan School of Public Health and the lead author of the study. The study also excludes onsite power generation, a rapidly growing trend that BloombergNEF estimates could increase overall U.S. emissions by 10% if all proposed projects are built. Nov 16, 2026 23:00 | Source: slashdot.org

AT&T is suing (PDF) Comcast over its "fiber-powered" advertising, arguing that the term misleads consumers into believing Xfinity offers fiber-to-the-home service when most customers actually receive their Internet through coaxial cable. "Comcast should be required to restore to AT&T any and all profits earned as a result of its false and misleading advertising or provide AT&T with any other restitutionary relief the Court deems appropriate," the lawsuit said. The lawsuit asked for a permanent injunction "requiring Comcast to cease describing its hybrid fiber-coaxial service as 'Fiber-Powered.'" Ars Technica reports: While Comcast has deployed plenty of fiber throughout the US, its cable Internet service makes the final connection to homes with coaxial cable. This results in upload speeds being far slower than download speeds. In contrast, fiber-to-the-home service can provide symmetrical upload and download speeds. Comcast has deployed fiber-to-the-home in only small portions of its cable territory while advertising its service as fiber-powered in the majority of areas where cable makes the final connection. AT&T's lawsuit said that in describing its cable broadband as fiber-powered, Comcast "conveys to reasonable consumers the misleading impression that Xfinity Internet is a fiber-to-the-home service comparable to AT&T Fiber. In fact, the vast majority of Xfinity Internet runs over a hybrid fiber-coaxial ('HFC') network, where the critical 'last mile' connection to the customer's home uses copper coaxial cable, not fiber." [...] AT&T previously challenged Comcast's fiber-powered claims through the advertising industry's self-regulatory system operated by BBB National Programs' National Advertising Division (NAD). The NAD determined in June that Comcast accurately described its mix of cable and fiber technology in a disclosure, but that Comcast didn't make the disclosure prominently enough. [...] The NAD recommended that Comcast make the disclosure "more prominent and more proximate to the 'fiber-powered' Wi-Fi claim as necessary to ensure that the disclosure is clear and conspicuous." Comcast said it would comply with the decision, but AT&T's lawsuit makes it clear that it isn't satisfied with the current state of Comcast's advertising.
