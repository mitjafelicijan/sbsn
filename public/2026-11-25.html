On the day of the network meltdown, L3Harris was undertaking a maintenance job that involved switching the system from a decades-old router to a new one. In the past, this kind of work typically involved at least six FAA employees. Under the Trump administration, it's now often coordinated by one employee: an FAA liaison. And there are only four people in the country with that title, according to sources familiar with the process. The liaison's job involves coordinating and scheduling these upgrades — not approving them, sources said... The Trump administration's effort to move maintenance work along faster also eliminated a layer of FAA review that was critical to assessing risk [according to a source familiar with the process]. Previously, local technical operations managers, site technicians and others would determine when the work could be done with the least impact on air traffic and to game out worst-case scenarios. The union contends that process did not happen before L3Harris arrived at the El Dorado, Arkansas, site on November 13... [David Spero, president of the union], contends the FAA has given private companies "too much authority and generally unfettered access." CNN also points out a 2009 incident where 129 of America's air traffic facilities experienced an outage of FAA telecommunications "that delayed thousands of travelers and grounded hundreds of flights nationwide. The detonation is back on. Nearly five tons of unstable lead azide explosives will be detonated Wednesday morning at a U.S. Army ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa, reports the Iowa Capital Dispatch (part of the nonprofit States Newsroom network "The scheduled time follows numerous delays over the past week from the Army, a lawsuit, intervention from the president, and widespread public concern of the health and environmental effects associated with detonating a lead-based explosive." Lead deposition maps released by the Army Tuesday morning show lead released by the detonation could be dispersed in the air as far as 100 miles southwest of Middletown and cross into Missouri. An additional map shows there is a potential for lead surface dust to settle as far as 50 miles southwest of the detonation site. The Army has recommended, and the Federal Emergency Management Agency is supporting, the evacuation of Iowans in Middletown and the immediate area — a 5-mile cone to the southwest of the detonation site — during the detonation period. Soldiers, environmental teams and other federal officials are in the area helping to sample soils and alert residents of the detonation plans... A slew of federal officials from the Army, the EPA, Department of Health and Human Services, Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms, Department of Agriculture and more gathered at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant Tuesday for a press conference and shared new information with the public about the response... Speaking at the press conference Tuesday, Sean Kaufman with HHS said the highest risk is for pregnant individuals, children, or people with chronic illnesses who live or plan to be in the areas the Army forecasts will be impacted. "You definitely want to be out of that area," Kaufman said of the at-risk groups. "I would evacuate that area that's signaled in the model... " As many as 100 Iowa National Guard soldiers will be available to aid local, state and federal agencies in their public safety plans [according to a news release from the governor]. Some of those mitigations include: sand around the structure holding the barrels; two walls of four, 16,000-gallon water tanks; a plan to "flood" the exterior of the storage area with aqueous foam to help absorb particulate matter from the blast; and modeling to forecast impacted areas based on weather conditions.
