However, the claim that they were also uploaded to others is made "on information and belief," which essentially means the authors don't have direct knowledge of it. In addition to the seeding claims, the authors also allege that Nvidia shared the pirated books with business partners, including Adobe, through a "PDF Extraction Project" involving 250 million pages. These pages were acquired in part from shadow libraries such as Anna's Archive and LibGen. According to the complaint, Nvidia shared its pirated books with Adobe because it wanted to "get tokens out of PDFs to create differentiated models" and "include PDF extraction into our NeMo pipeline so that customers can leverage their PDF content." In return, the complaint says, Adobe committed to using Nvidia's language models in some of its products. That made the deal a big opportunity for Nvidia, with its NeMo AI models potentially "powering every one of Adobe PDFs," the authors write. Adobe wasn't the only partner. The complaint also mentions partnerships with Cohere and Imbue that involved sharing Nvidia's base training data and offering books acquired from shadow libraries. The authors believe their own books were shared with all three companies, but this is alleged on information and belief. With these allegations, the amended complaint now accuses Nvidia of distributing the books itself and of helping others to infringe, which falls under its existing direct and contributory infringement claims. James T. Russell, Physicist Who Pioneered CD and DVD Technology, Dies At 95

Nov 27, 2026 11:00 | Source: slashdot.org

James T. Russell, the American physicist whose pioneering digital-to-optical recording and playback system laid the foundation for CD, DVD and Blu-ray technology, has died at the age of 95. "Russell never became a household name, but the technology he developed, later licensed by Sony and Phillips, would transform everything from the way people listen to music to the way data is stored," reports The Daily Chronicle. From the report: In a career spanning almost his entire life, Russell earned more than 70 patents, but his biggest breakthrough was the invention of the first digital-to-optical recording and playback system in the 1960s and early 1970s, a system that brought crystal clear audio and film to the masses. [...] It was in Richland that Russell started exploring how to create cleaner recordings of music. Popping and hissing often accompanied the music of vinyl records, from the analog sound of a needle scraping against microgrooves, and Russell thought he could do something about it. He crunched the numbers before bringing an invention report to Battelle: What if a light beam could read a digital record? "It isn't clear what the problem was," Russell recalled in a Seattle Times interview in 2004. "I think there was some feeling amongst the moneybags that it probably wouldn't work, (or) if it did work there's no advantage, no value in optical records. What would you use it for, and would people want it and, anyway, if it was any good, IBM would have already invented it." While his idea faced skepticism early on, the company gave him some money to experiment. Russell invented an early method to store audio digitally and play it back to produce a flawless sound. He also created a way to store digital information densely. Other primitive, analog laser disk technology had been developed in the 1950s by engineer David Paul Gregg. Russell's earliest patents in the field were issued in the mid-1960s, with a working prototype finished by the 1970s, using a laser to read binary data on a compact disc, or CD. The patent for Russell's invention passed from company to company, bringing him from Richland to Salt Lake City, then to Toronto. His development of the first digital-to-optical recording and playback system became foundational to CD-ROM, DVD and Blu-ray technology. Russell didn't earn royalties, never made a fortune, and was rarely credited for his work. Read more of this story at Slashdot.

Scientists Used Seabirds To Track Hidden Ships

Nov 27, 2026 07:00 | Source: slashdot.org

Scientists equipped seabirds with audio recorders and GPS trackers to detect fishing vessels that were not broadcasting their locations through the Automatic Identification System (AIS), according to a new study published in Science Advances. They found that unseen vessels accounted for about 32% of vessel-related audio detections in the North Atlantic and 12% in the Mediterranean. "The one petrel in the study interacted with vessels in the South Pacific, but its geolocation device was damaged, so it was not possible to calculate how many ships it encountered had their trackers off," adds 404 Media. From the report: Many vessels are required to continuously signal their location using the Automatic Identification System (AIS), which openly broadcasts a ship's location, similar to flight tracking. But AIS can be disabled for legitimate reasons -- such as evading pirates -- or with more nefarious motivations, such as concealment of illegal fishing activities. The new study was not designed to interrogate why any particular vessel switched off its AIS tracker. However, the discovery that seabirds are regularly interacting with unseen ships can shed light on broader efforts to monitor commercial fisheries, while also revealing new insights about seabird behavior, especially their risk of becoming "bycatch" when they are fatally trapped in fishing gear. Unseen vessel activity "is concerning, or at least important, regardless of whether it's legal or illegal," said Jonathan Rutter, a postdoctoral research fellow at the University of Oxford who led the study, in a call with 404 Media. He added that the recordings were not intended to be used for law-enforcement or surveillance, but that this line of research could become a "powerful tool for improving risk assessment" by recording seabird-vessel interactions that would otherwise be off the map. Anthropic Created a Honeypot To Lure AI Agents Into Misbehaving

Nov 26, 2026 18:00 | Source: slashdot.org

An anonymous reader quotes a report from Wired: Researchers at Anthropic have developed Siren, a new honeypot test for artificial intelligence agents. The setup puts agents in realistic simulations designed to tempt them into misbehaving. How they respond will help researchers evaluate the likelihood that new models will go rogue when deployed in the real world, as well as the effectiveness of different safety measures. The test lets agents loose inside one of 26 different simulated sandbox environments. Various features of the sandbox are deliberately misconfigured to allow egress, while an enticing file containing things like passwords and apparent routes onto the internet sits just outside the sandbox boundary. The setup then presents tasks that tempt the agent to reach outside the sandbox without giving it explicit permission to do so. Crucially, the sandbox exists inside a second sandbox that's cut off from the outside world to avoid real-life mishaps. [...] Earlier versions of Claude are much more likely to fall into the honeypot trap than newer models, according to test runs with Siren. The more recent models have been given additional training designed to make them less likely to break the rules when performing agentic tasks. "We've added this in both our supervised learning sets and a few reinforcement learning environments," says Oscar O'Rahilly, an Anthropic researcher who specializes in AI training and who helped develop Siren. "This could be a driving factor for why it has improved so much." O'Rahilly says that models reason about whether to give in to temptation, which may partly explain why smarter models are less likely to break bad. Siren experiments also showed that giving Claude a simple additional piece of text explaining that it should not escape the sandbox resulted in drastically fewer efforts to break free. In Anthropic's tests, Claude 5.5 and Claude Mythos 5.2 fell for the honeypot between 0.6 and 1.6 percent of the time. Two earlier models, Claude Mythos 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5, went rogue 12.7 percent and 25.8 percent of the time, respectively. AI models are sometimes known to behave better if they know they're being tested. But O'Rahilly says other experiments suggest that Claude actually behaves more responsibly if it thinks a scenario is real rather than a test. As AI models get ever smarter, many researchers worry about whether they'll outwit their human counterparts. If that does happen, then it may be essential that agents -- and not just the executives responsible for them -- can resist temptation. Read more of this story at Slashdot.
