[$] Added indirection allows for larger BPF programs

Nov 19, 2026 17:32 | Source: lwn.net

The BPF verifier has historically worked by tracing every possible path of execution through a program, checking, at each instruction, that various safety properties hold. This works, but it can result in an exponential increase in the number of states that need checking in programs with complicated control-flow. Alexei Starovoitov, one of the kernel's core BPF maintainers, has shared a patch set that substantially reworks how the verifier tracks states, in order to reduce the size of that exponential explosion.

Microsoft Testing Remote Lock For Lost Or Stolen Windows Devices

Nov 19, 2026 16:00 | Source: slashdot.org

joshuark quotes a report from BleepingComputer: Microsoft is testing a new feature that lets enterprise IT administrators remotely lock stolen or lost Windows devices without wiping them. Named Remote Lock, this new feature is shipping to devices upgraded to Windows 11 Insider Preview builds in the Beta (Build 26320.10003) and Experimental (Build 26340.10013) channels. Admins can use Microsoft Intune custom configuration profiles to lock Microsoft Entra-joined, hybrid-joined, or MDM-enrolled devices that run Windows Enterprise, Education, or Pro. After the IT department remotely locks the device, the system can only be unlocked via Intune, since PINs, passwords, local admins, or web sign-ins will no longer work. Microsoft added that the user's credentials will remain valid and can still be used to sign into other devices that haven't been locked. "Windows signs out every active session, hides all sign-in options and shows a locked screen," said Windows Insider communications lead Stephen Lines. "Nothing is erased: the device stays enrolled and managed, BitLocker stays on and user data stays in place. The lock holds across restarts and while offline, and only an admin can lift it remotely, so the user picks up where they left off." Read more of this story at Slashdot.

Satellites Now Outnumber the Visible Stars Above Most Towns

Nov 19, 2026 07:00 | Source: slashdot.org

Satellites now outnumber visible stars in the night sky above many towns and cities, according to a non-peer-reviewed analysis by engineering firm Accu Components. The researchers estimated that roughly 710 satellites pass over an average U.K. town at any given moment, with about 105 illuminated by sunlight and potentially visible to the naked eye. Space.com reports: Although the researchers specifically focused on the U.K., they say that such heavy satellite traffic flows above most of Europe, the U.S. and other mid-latitude regions, especially between 40 and 50 degrees north or south. "Most Starlink satellites orbit at around 53 degrees to the equator, so the satellites bunch up over the mid-latitudes. These worldwide figures are approximate, based on a global grid over a single day," Accu Components spokesperson told Space.com in an email. "Skies near the equator are the quietest, with around 490 satellites." Not all of those satellites are visible to the naked eye, the researchers said in a statement. Because satellites unlike stars don't produce their own light, they must be illuminated by the sun, while the ground below has already sunk into darkness, to be visible. [...] The spokesperson said that the actual number of visible satellites varies drastically throughout the night. During the hours after dusk and before dawn, a much greater number of satellites can be seen, while in the middle of the night most no longer catch any light. "For a typical U.K. town, around 300 satellites are catching the sunlight in the first hour or so after dark," the spokesperson said. "That falls to fewer than 20 around midnight, then climbs back to around 300 before dawn." New Study Shows How Young People Feel About AI Companions

Nov 19, 2026 03:30 | Source: slashdot.org

An anonymous reader quotes a report from 404 Media: A new study from Gallup looked at how young people across the world are making friends, both digitally and in person. The good news is that around 93% of the young people surveyed reported having at least one strong in-person friendship. The weird news is that 33% said they had an AI companion. Gallup's study is lengthy and nuanced. It partnered with the Boston Children's Digital Wellness Lab to conduct the study and used online surveys of more than 16,000 young people from the ages of 15 to 22 in 16 different countries. A lot of the topline results won't surprise anyone living in today's constantly online digital-forward age. Gallup found that respondents felt their in-person friendships were their strongest, that digital friendships complemented rather than replaced in-person ones, and that stronger friendships made people happier no matter how the connection was made. Three things that stood out were how the young respondents talked about AI, loneliness, and doomscrolling. Young people in the United States, Canada, and UK reported the least interest in AI overall, with only 20% saying they used it once a day or more. Almost 40 percent of young people in Brazil use it daily. Japan and Italy both come in around 35%. "Nearly half of young people in the United States, United Kingdom and Canada have used AI no more than a few times in the past month, including those who have not used it at all," the researchers wrote. Most of the respondents (82%) said they'd used AI to look up information, 68% asked it for advice, and 33% used it as a companion. Young people with online-only friends were more likely to use AI for companionship, "although these patterns do not establish whether AI companionship supplements or substitutes for human relationships," they wrote. The researchers discovered that people in different countries felt wildly different about their AI buddies. Almost half of Australians with AI friends rated the relationship as "high quality" and only 12% of young people in Poland did. Mostly, though, people thought of their AI friends as mid: "In most countries [...] young people are more likely to give their AI companionship a medium rating than either a high or low rating." People who used AI as a friend also reported higher levels of loneliness, the study found. "However, the findings cannot determine whether young people turn to AI because they feel lonely, whether AI companionship influences their social experiences or whether other circumstances help explain these relationships," the researchers wrote. [...] The study's authors warned about reading too much into these results. "These assessments should be compared cautiously with those for human friendships examined in this report, as young people may have different expectations of what makes an AI companion or human friendship meaningful or supportive," the researchers wrote. "The broader message is not that one form of connection is inherently better or worse, but that the quality of young people's relationships and the wider social context in which they exist matter greatly," the study concluded. Read more of this story at Slashdot.
