Earth has a New Moon

Nov 21, 2026 17:00 | Source: slashdot.org

"Earth is currently hosting a second moon," reports CNN: Our planet's gravity captured the tiny asteroid in April. This temporary "mini-moon" — about 2 meters (6 feet) in diameter — will stick around until early December, when it's expected to leave Earth's sphere of influence and continue on a path around the sun. The object is so small and dim in observations that astronomers needed several weeks to confirm it wasn't a defunct satellite or a fragment of one. The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center — which tracks asteroids, comets and other small bodies in the solar system — named the newcomer 2026 QQ8 in late October. While mini-moon is not an official designation, astronomers have previously used the moniker to characterize a handful of natural objects, including 2006 RH120, which spent around one year in Earth orbit, and 2020 CD3, which orbited our planet for about three years. However, it's not uncommon for Earth to have one of these transient companions. "On average, there's probably one there at any given time," said Matthew Payne, director of the Minor Planet Center. "They hang around for a few weeks or months. Sometimes it's none, sometimes it's a couple." The catalog of these visitors is relatively short because they usually slip under the radar... The exact origin of the space rock is "an active area of inquiry," Payne said, but most NEOs originate from the asteroid belt, a region between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter that contains millions of rocky objects of varying size that revolve around the sun. Over millions of years, gravitational forces can shift some of these objects' orbits toward the inner solar system. Read more of this story at Slashdot.

Mercury-Bound X-Ray Telescope Delivers First Images After Eight Years In Space

Nov 21, 2026 11:00 | Source: slashdot.org

A British-built X-ray telescope aboard the European-Japanese BepiColombo spacecraft has captured its first astronomical images after eight years in space and roughly 6.2 billion miles of travel. Phys.org reports: A team led by the University of Leicester has been making calibration measurements with the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS), one of the instruments on the two-spacecraft BepiColombo mission to Mercury, which begins its orbital insertion tomorrow (Nov. 21) in preparation for the final stage of arrival at our solar system's smallest planet. After an eight-year journey, it is vital that the team checks how MIXS is performing. Although they have been monitoring the instrument during its trip, it was only in September, following the separation of the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) from the joined science orbiters, Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (MMO) or Mio, that MIXS could finally see into space for the first time and calibration measurements could be made before the orbit insertion sequence begins. The Crab Nebula is 6,500 light-years away and a well-characterized astrophysical source of X-ray light. By observing it, the team can work out the performance characteristics of MIXS in space and compare these with calibration measurements made with the same instrument in the laboratory in 2015. These observations provide MIXS's first view of an astronomical X-ray source. The main observation sequence consisted of 55 spacecraft slews, with 39 hours of observations during Sept. 11-12. Measuring how much the Crab Nebula's image is spread across MIXS's detector helps the scientists measure MIXS's angular resolution, or how sharply it will be able to distinguish features on Mercury's surface. They found this to be slightly better than when it was last measured in the lab in 2015. "During our calibration observations we have determined that MIXS is generally performing fantastically well in space! We have no effects in our images that are associated with 'stray light' (optical photons getting in when they should not). We continue to see effects of galactic cosmic rays as we did during cruise, but we understand these and can filter them out," said Professor Emma Bunch, Principal Investigator (PI) for the MIXS instrument and Director of the Institute for Space at the University of Leicester. "We see some self-fluorescence which appears when the sun is illuminating the optic, but this won't affect our dayside observations of Mercury's surface. And the detector has suffered no radiation damage after eight years in space. We see some effects in the instrument that we are still studying and expect to undertake some optimization procedures during the Mercury commissioning phase to fine-tune MIXS in preparation for the science phase."
