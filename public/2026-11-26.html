Anthropic Created a Honeypot To Lure AI Agents Into Misbehaving

Nov 26, 2026 18:00 | Source: slashdot.org

An anonymous reader quotes a report from Wired: Researchers at Anthropic have developed Siren, a new honeypot test for artificial intelligence agents. The setup puts agents in realistic simulations designed to tempt them into misbehaving. How they respond will help researchers evaluate the likelihood that new models will go rogue when deployed in the real world, as well as the effectiveness of different safety measures. The test lets agents loose inside one of 26 different simulated sandbox environments. Various features of the sandbox are deliberately misconfigured to allow egress, while an enticing file containing things like passwords and apparent routes onto the internet sits just outside the sandbox boundary. The setup then presents tasks that tempt the agent to reach outside the sandbox without giving it explicit permission to do so. Crucially, the sandbox exists inside a second sandbox that's cut off from the outside world to avoid real-life mishaps. [...] Earlier versions of Claude are much more likely to fall into the honeypot trap than newer models, according to test runs with Siren. The more recent models have been given additional training designed to make them less likely to break the rules when performing agentic tasks. "We've added this in both our supervised learning sets and a few reinforcement learning environments," says Oscar O'Rahilly, an Anthropic researcher who specializes in AI training and who helped develop Siren. "This could be a driving factor for why it has improved so much." O'Rahilly says that models reason about whether to give in to temptation, which may partly explain why smarter models are less likely to break bad. Siren experiments also showed that giving Claude a simple additional piece of text explaining that it should not escape the sandbox resulted in drastically fewer efforts to break free. In Anthropic's tests, Claude 5.5 and Claude Mythos 5.2 fell for the honeypot between 0.6 and 1.6 percent of the time. Two earlier models, Claude Mythos 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5, went rogue 12.7 percent and 25.8 percent of the time, respectively. AI models are sometimes known to behave better if they know they're being tested. But O'Rahilly says other experiments suggest that Claude actually behaves more responsibly if it thinks a scenario is real rather than a test. As AI models get ever smarter, many researchers worry about whether they'll outwit their human counterparts. If that does happen, then it may be essential that agents -- and not just the executives responsible for them -- can resist temptation. Read more of this story at Slashdot.

Physicists Send an Unblockable Message Using Gravity

Nov 26, 2026 11:00 | Source: slashdot.org

Longtime Slashdot reader fahrbot-bot shares a report from Phys.org: University of Queensland researchers have used gravity to transmit a message through a brick wall using an elevator and a sensitive gravimeter. The unusual experiment took place in UQ's Parnell Building, where the team of physicists sent the elevator back and forth between two levels, moving its 1.8 metric ton (4,000 pound) counterweight between positions representing binary 0 and 1. A gravimeter in an adjacent bathroom detected the changes in the gravitational field and translated the message into a single word, "Gravity." The research has been published in Science Advances. "Unlike the electromagnetic signals used in modern wireless communication, a gravitational signal cannot simply be screened out by placing ordinary material in its path," [said Dr. Charles Woffinden from the School of Mathematics and Physics]. "While the present system is far from practical, it demonstrates the possibility of communicating using a method that ordinary physical barriers cannot block." Professor Andrew White said the biggest limitation to future gravity-based communication was its slow speed. "Our elevator was snail-paced -- it's from the 1950s -- so our experiment transmitted just 1 bit per minute, meaning it took an hour to send a single word," White said. "The speed of gravity communication would come down to how big the mass is, how fast you can move it and the sensitivity of the detector. Our experiment used a classical gravity sensor, but our modeling indicates that quantum sensors could, with rather ambitious assumptions, support ranges of up to about 500 meters (1,640 feet) and data rates of around 15 bits per minute."
