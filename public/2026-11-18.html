School Phone Bans Are Gradually Improving Test Scores

Nov 17, 2026 19:00 | Source: slashdot.org

An anonymous reader quotes a report from The New York Times: School cellphone bans are beginning to modestly improve students' academic performance, according to the first large national analysis that compared students' test scores before and after the bans. Schools with strict bans -- requiring students to lock their phones in pouches all school day -- saw math and reading scores improve as if students had an additional week or two of school a year, the study found. It was published Tuesday by the Center for Education Policy Research at Harvard. U.S. students' test scores began falling over a decade ago. The drop coincided with the rise of smartphones, raising questions about their role in the decline. The evidence so far on the academic effects of phone bans has been mixed. But the new study indicates they are starting to have an influence. The study looked at 3.3 million students between 2021 and 2026. They were in third through 10th grade, attending 4,600 U.S. schools. Using scores on MAP tests, which track achievement from fall to spring, it compared the performance of students at schools that put in strict phone bans with those at similar schools that did not have such bans. The annual gains reversed about half the annual loss that an average student experienced in the years leading up to the pandemic. (Students lost even more ground during pandemic closures.) "The earlier studies on cellphone bans have been dismissed as implying small impacts, and in-school cellphone use has been ruled out as playing a big role in the decline," said Thomas Kane, a professor at the Harvard Graduate School of Education and an author of the study. "But we think that's mistaken." Read more of this story at Slashdot.

BugMeNot Calls It Quits

Nov 17, 2026 17:30 | Source: slashdot.org

BugMeNot, the password-sharing service launched in 2003 to help users bypass website registration requirements, has shut down without explanation after more than two decades. All that's left on the website is a farewell message that reads: "Thanks to everyone who envisioned a better internet." heise online reports: Founded by Guy King, the service had been online since 2003: BugMeNot was a collection of login credentials for various online services. Volunteers submitted credentials they had created for different websites. The idea was simple: Nobody wants to create a full account and provide all their sensitive information just to use a service occasionally. Two decades ago, this privacy concern was more firmly embedded in the public consciousness, but it has since faded considerably. Back then, usernames and passwords were usually the only access controls, so people could easily submit those credentials. Multifactor authentication was still exotic, and passkeys were a distant prospect. The once-popular service lost its shine after just a few years. Service providers quickly discovered and blocked most of the credentials. They could also prevent users from saving logins for their services directly through BugMeNot, for example when those services were paid. More modern security mechanisms also emerged in recent years. As a result, credentials found on BugMeNot rarely worked. Most online providers also blocked the service's disposable email addresses under @bugmenot.com, making them rarely usable. Many people forgot about the site. Now its operators have shut it down without giving a reason. Additional discussions about BugMeNot's closure can be found on Reddit and Hacker News. Redict deprecated in favor of Valkey

Nov 17, 2026 16:20 | Source: lwn.net

Drew DeVault has announced that Redict, a fork of Redis that was created in response to Redis's 2024 license change, has been deprecated. He recommends switching to Valkey, the permissively licensed Redis fork supported by the Linux Foundation. The two projects are mutually compatible, so switching should be as simple as changing which package is installed and renaming the configuration file. I had hoped to rally the Redis diaspora around a copyleft initiative, to protect ourselves from future rug-pulls, and made Redict to serve this purpose. However, with the valkey project's success, and Redis's return to a copyleft license, Redict was not able to build a sustainable community. We recommend that Redict users switch to valkey, which uses a permissive license but has better long-term prospects. We advise against switching back to Redis; though it is once again open source, and copyleft, it uses a contributor license agreement which will facilitate another rug-pull when Redis has a bad financial quarter.
